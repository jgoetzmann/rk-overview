Cortex-M0+ constant-multiply costs collected 2026-09-01 14:42 CT, cycle 800

Arm documents two implementation-time multiplier options for Cortex-M0+: a fast single-cycle array multiplier and a small 32-cycle iterative multiplier. MULS computes the low 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product; ordinary adds, subtracts, and immediate or register shifts take one cycle under the manual’s zero-wait-state assumptions. Thus a single generic Cortex-M0+ multiplication cost is not valid: on the fast variant, replacing MULS with several shift/add instructions normally increases instruction cycles, whereas on the iterative variant a short shift/add sequence can be substantially cheaper. Loads, branches, pipeline effects, and memory wait states must be accounted for separately.

  • Cortex-M0+ has selectable single-cycle and 32-cycle MULS implementations.
  • MULS returns only the least-significant 32 product bits.
  • Arm lists basic add, subtract, and shift instructions as one cycle with zero wait states.
  • Q15 products of two signed 16-bit values fit in a signed 32-bit intermediate.
  • An arithmetic right shift sign-extends and naturally implements floor division by a power of two.
  • CSD represents constants with digits -1, 0, and 1 and minimizes nonzero signed digits.
  • CSD often reduces additions versus ordinary binary expansion but does not guarantee the shortest software instruction sequence.
  • Constant loading, register pressure, reused intermediates, and final Q15 rescaling affect realized cycle cost.

Search-based optimization of low-order RK tableaus collected 2026-09-01 14:28 CT, cycle 800

Classical Runge–Kutta order conditions are nonlinear polynomial constraints on the Butcher coefficients. Explicit methods have about s(s+1)/2 coefficients, and orders one through four can be attained with s=p stages. Additional stages therefore create degrees of freedom that can be used to reduce principal truncation-error norms, enlarge a relevant stability region, satisfy low-storage constraints, or construct embedded formulas. The literature treats these goals as constrained optimization problems, commonly using multistart searches because the feasible sets are nonconvex and contain many local minima.

RK-Opt separates some designs into optimization of a stability polynomial followed by recovery of a compatible tableau; it can also minimize principal error norms subject to order, stability-polynomial, and implementation constraints. RKTK similarly automates rooted-tree enumeration, Butcher-weight evaluation, derivatives, order-condition residuals, and parallel nonlinear searches. Published comparisons caution that principal error constants are directly comparable only between methods of the same order, and application tests can diverge from rankings based on asymptotic constants or stability alone. Consequently, the stated cross-order hypotheses require held-out measurements at the actual cycle budget: neither formal order nor stage count by itself determines the winner, especially because the consulted optimization literature does not model Q15 floor rounding.

  • Orders up to four are attainable with the same number of explicit stages as the order.
  • Extra stages supply free coefficients for objectives beyond satisfying order conditions.
  • Principal error norm, stability extent, storage form, and embedded estimation are distinct optimization objectives.
  • Tableau searches are nonlinear and nonconvex, so multistart numerical optimization is common.
  • RK-Opt supports order constraints, stability-polynomial targets, error minimization, and implementation constraints.
  • RKTK computes rooted-tree order residuals and gradients for parallel searches.
  • Principal error constants alone do not justify comparisons between different formal orders.
  • Q15 floor-rounding behavior must be evaluated separately from real-arithmetic order conditions.

Quantized Runge–Kutta error and implementation collected 2026-09-01 07:18 CT, cycle 750

Fixed-point ODE studies report that rounding mode can materially change solver behavior. Hopkins et al. found that GCC's default downward rounding for fixed-point constants and multiplication harmed accuracy; round-to-nearest improved results, while stochastic rounding generally reduced systematic drift further. In their neural ODE experiments, six random residual bits were sufficient for stochastic rounding, but replacing round-to-nearest added about 30% overhead in an RK2 benchmark. Thus stochastic rounding may be unsuitable for a strict Cortex-M0+ cycle budget, while floor rounding creates a directional error that should be evaluated explicitly rather than modeled as zero-mean noise.

Runge–Kutta literature also shows that stage perturbations are propagated according to a method's internal stability and even its algebraic implementation. More stages provide more sites for quantization, but order and stage count alone do not determine the resulting error: algebraically equivalent implementations can have different internal-error amplification. Explicit solver reduction can simultaneously remove operations and preserve more fixed-point precision. These findings support testing the stated order/stage hypotheses under the exact Q15 evaluation graph, scaling, saturation, and floor operations; higher formal order does not by itself imply lower held-out fixed-point error. Arm's CMSIS-DSP documentation additionally confirms that standard Q15 multiplication saturates outside the representable range, making saturation events a separate nonlinear failure mode from ordinary rounding accumulation.

  • Floor or downward rounding introduces systematic error rather than zero-mean perturbations.
  • Round-to-nearest and stochastic rounding substantially improved fixed-point ODE results in published experiments.
  • Stochastic rounding carried about 30% overhead over round-to-nearest in one RK2 benchmark.
  • Six random residual bits were sufficient in the tested stochastic-rounding ODE solvers.
  • Additional RK stages add quantization sites, but internal amplification depends on coefficients and implementation.
  • Algebraically equivalent Runge–Kutta implementations can propagate stage errors differently.
  • Explicit solver reduction can reduce operation count while improving fixed-point range and precision.
  • Q15 saturation must be measured separately from nonsaturating rounding error.

Long-run roundoff in fixed-point ODE solvers collected 2026-09-01 02:14 CT, cycle 700

Probabilistic studies of deterministic ODE solvers model accumulated rounding error as a random process whose distribution depends on time, step size, and arithmetic precision. Experiments with Euler, classical RK4, VODE, and RADAU5 show that dynamically amplified rounding errors can produce divergent long-time trajectories. For unbiased stochastic rounding, backward-error results often replace worst-case growth proportional to n·u with high-probability growth proportional to √n·u; limited-random-bit implementations, however, introduce bias. These conclusions do not transfer directly to floor rounding, which is systematically directed rather than zero-mean and can therefore accumulate drift instead of cancellation.

Runge–Kutta roundoff also depends on within-step error propagation. Internal-stability polynomials quantify how perturbations at individual stages reach the step output, and algebraically equivalent implementations can have very different amplification factors. Thus, increasing the stage count from four to six adds quantization events and cost but need not worsen accuracy if the tableau and evaluation form suppress their propagation; order alone does not determine the outcome. Fixed-point ODE experiments likewise find that rounding mode, coefficient representation, solver formulation, and problem parameters materially affect accuracy, while stochastic rounding can outperform round-to-nearest at reduced precision. Under a fixed cycle budget and Q15 floor arithmetic, comparisons between candidate cells therefore require measuring both truncation error and signed quantization drift over the actual number of affordable steps.

  • Truncation error decreases with step size until accumulated arithmetic error becomes comparable.
  • Zero-mean probabilistic roundoff commonly gives √n-type growth, whereas deterministic worst-case bounds allow n-type growth.
  • Floor rounding is biased, so stochastic-rounding cancellation bounds are not directly applicable.
  • Limited-bit stochastic rounding can itself become biased.
  • Runge–Kutta stage residuals are amplified according to internal-stability polynomials.
  • Internal error propagation depends on the implemented algebraic form, not only the Butcher tableau.
  • Additional stages create more rounding sites but can still win if truncation reduction and internal stability compensate.
  • Fixed-point solver accuracy is sensitive to coefficient quantization, rounding rules, and problem regime.

Low-precision integration under real-time budgets collected 2026-08-31 08:13 CT, cycle 650

Hard real-time simulation literature and current engineering guidance favor fixed-step, fixed-cost solvers because their execution time is predictable; solver choice must jointly account for computational cost, stiffness, and accuracy. Reduced-precision Runge–Kutta research reports that naive low-precision implementations can accumulate rounding error as the step size decreases. Compensated summation, mixed precision, and unbiased stochastic rounding can mitigate this behavior, but deterministic floor rounding is biased and therefore does not inherit the guarantees associated with unbiased stochastic rounding.

Mixed-precision RK studies show that accuracy and runtime depend on the tableau, precision assigned to individual operations, solver parameters, and hardware kernels. Consequently, formal order alone does not determine performance at a fixed cycle budget: a six-stage fourth-order method may obtain better stability or error constants than classical four-stage RK4, while also performing more quantized operations and permitting fewer steps. Energy-preserving continuous-stage RK methods provide algebraic conservation properties for Hamiltonian systems, but the published constructions commonly add implicit, continuous-stage, or quadrature work; moreover, exact-arithmetic preservation is limited by finite-precision roundoff. The literature therefore supports comparing the two archive cells using their realized step counts, saturation and floor-error statistics, stability behavior, and long-horizon invariant drift, rather than stage count or nominal order alone.

  • Naive reduced-precision RK can show increasing roundoff error as step size decreases.
  • Floor rounding is biased, unlike the unbiased stochastic rounding analyzed as a mitigation.
  • Mixed-precision RK performance depends on both method parameters and hardware implementation.
  • Equal formal order does not imply equal error at an equal cycle budget.
  • Extra stages can improve stability or truncation constants but also add quantized operations and reduce the available step count.

Cortex-M0+ fixed-point multiplication costs collected 2026-08-30 23:00 CT, cycle 600

Arm documents two Cortex-M0+ multiplier implementations: a fast single-cycle array and a small 32-cycle iterative multiplier. MULS returns only the least-significant 32 bits. By contrast, register additions, subtractions, and immediate shifts normally take one cycle, so shift-add constant multiplication can be substantially cheaper on the small-multiplier variant but usually cannot beat one-cycle MULS on the fast variant. GCC therefore has a distinct cortex-m0plus.small-multiply cost model and, at -O2, tests that some constant products are expanded without MULS; its -Os tests show that code-size optimization can make a different choice.

CSD represents a constant with signed nonzero digits, turning multiplication into shifted copies combined by addition or subtraction. This can reduce the operation count relative to ordinary binary expansion, although shared subexpressions and coefficient structure can permit further improvements beyond independent CSD forms. For Q15 floor rounding, Cortex-M0+ ASR is directly relevant: Arm specifies that arithmetic right shift divides a signed value by a power of two and rounds toward negative infinity, and ASRS costs one cycle. Consequently, a slow-multiplier cycle model can favor sparse shift-add RK coefficients, while a six-stage method still incurs more coefficient applications than a four-stage method; whether its error advantage survives the fixed budget depends on the actual coefficient encodings, reused intermediates, and selected MCU multiplier implementation.

  • Cortex-M0+ MULS costs either 1 or 32 cycles, depending on the implemented multiplier.
  • MULS produces the low 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product.
  • ADD, SUB, immediate shifts, and ASRS are listed as one-cycle instructions.
  • ASR implements signed power-of-two division rounded toward negative infinity.
  • CSD constant multiplication uses shifted positive and negative copies of the input.

Numerical optimization of low-order Runge–Kutta tableaus collected 2026-08-30 20:19 CT, cycle 550

Butcher’s rooted-tree theory turns a requested RK order into polynomial equality constraints on the tableau coefficients. Low-order families can retain free parameters after these constraints are imposed: the two-stage, second-order family is a standard example, with Heun, midpoint, and Ralston methods obtained by different parameter choices. Classical optimization minimizes norms of principal local-error coefficients; the optimum depends on the selected error measure and, for higher orders, whether the target is a scalar equation or a system.

Modern searches treat tableau construction as constrained numerical optimization. RKTK and RungeKuttaToolKit automate rooted-tree enumeration, Butcher weights, derivatives, residual minimization, and parallel searches. Other work uses interval constraint programming to enclose genuine solutions and global optimization to minimize truncation error, while RK-Opt separates stability-polynomial optimization from realization as a tableau. Low-storage studies add structural constraints and jointly assess truncation error, stability, memory, and implementation efficiency.

These sources optimize real-valued coefficients and conventional arithmetic properties; they do not directly model Q15 coefficient representability, floor rounding at each operation, Cortex-M0+ instruction costs, or a fixed cycle budget. For that setting, the literature supports enforcing order conditions earliest and searching remaining degrees of freedom, but the objective and feasibility tests must additionally evaluate the quantized operational scheme because rounded coefficients need not preserve the exact real-arithmetic order conditions.

  • Order conditions are polynomial constraints indexed by rooted trees.
  • Low-order RK families often leave free parameters after satisfying the requested order.
  • Minimizing principal error coefficients is a classical way to select among equivalent-order methods.
  • Different norms and problem classes can produce different error-optimal coefficients.
  • RKTK supplies parallel nonlinear searches; RungeKuttaToolKit supplies Butcher-weight residuals and derivatives.
  • Interval constraint programming can certify enclosures containing coefficients that satisfy order conditions.
  • Stability-polynomial optimization can be convex even when realization as a full RK tableau is not.
  • Low-storage constructions impose additional coefficient structure and trade accuracy against stability and memory.

Quantized Runge–Kutta on Cortex-M0+ collected 2026-08-30 17:32 CT, cycle 549

Runge–Kutta roundoff is not merely an independent per-step disturbance: perturbations introduced in stage calculations propagate both within a step and across later steps, and their amplification depends on the RK coefficients and the precise implementation. In Q15 arithmetic, flooring every rescaled product produces an error in the interval from minus one quantization unit up to zero, creating directional bias rather than the cancellation expected from zero-mean errors. Repeated small updates may consequently drift or disappear entirely, while saturation introduces a separate nonlinear error. Wider intermediates and delayed narrowing can reduce the number of quantization points without changing the nominal RK formula.

Research on low-precision Runge–Kutta discretizations of the heat equation found correlated error growth and eventual stagnation under deterministic round-to-nearest, whereas stochastic rounding made leading errors zero-mean and substantially reduced accumulation. Those results do not directly establish performance for general fixed-point ODEs or floor rounding, but they show why a consistently biased rule deserves separate analysis. On Cortex-M0+, stochastic rounding also requires extra state and operations, while multiply latency is implementation-dependent: the core can contain either a single-cycle or a 32-cycle 32-bit multiplier. Thus fixed cycle budgets must be checked against the particular MCU and compiled instruction sequence, not the architecture name alone.

  • Floor quantization has a one-sided error and therefore introduces systematic negative bias.
  • RK stage perturbations can be amplified within a single step, depending on coefficients and implementation form.
  • Accumulated perturbations can dominate truncation error as the step size is reduced.
  • Small quantized increments can vanish, causing stagnation or frozen state components.
  • Stochastic rounding removes earliest-order rounding bias in expectation but adds random-number and comparison costs.
  • Q15 uses 16-bit storage with 15 fractional bits; wider intermediates can postpone narrowing and saturation.
  • Saturation and rounding are distinct error mechanisms and should be measured separately.
  • Cortex-M0+ implementations may have either one-cycle or 32-cycle multiplication, materially affecting cycle estimates.

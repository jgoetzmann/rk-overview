What the explicit search found, at snapshot 2026-09-10.

At a fixed budget of 65,536 cycles on a Cortex-M0+ cost model, the search found Runge-Kutta methods with up to 2.95× lower held-out error than the best classical method. The data also shows why: Q15's floor rounding adds a bias of about half an LSB to every multiply, and that alone reorders the classical methods before any search starts. The anchor result

rk4 and rk38 are both four-stage, order-4 methods with the same stability polynomial, and textbooks treat them as interchangeable. Priced against a real multiplier they are not. With a 1-cycle multiplier rk4 is cheaper (33 against 36 cycles per step). With a 32-cycle multiplier rk38 is cheaper (64 against 85), because rk4's coefficients need more shift-add work. Which one to ship depends on the multiplier the chip has.

1Discovered methods lead in all 16 cells

The archive keeps one elite per MAP-Elites cell (order, stage count, cycle-cost bucket). 18 cells are occupied: 16 by discovered methods and 2 by classical ones (euler and rk38). Each discovered cell is compared with every classical method of equal or lower per-step cost, since a cheaper method could always be swapped in. All 16 discovered cells have the lower held-out error. The median error ratio, discovered over classical, is 0.71, and the best is 0.34.

The best discovered method is a 3-stage, order-2 tableau at 22 cycles per step, with held-out error 0.0286 against 0.0844 for midpoint, the best classical method. Every coefficient is dyadic, so the method needs only shifts and adds.

Its tableau and its error on each held-out problem

A = [[0, 0, 0], [13/16, 0, 0], [5/32, 11/32, 0]], b = (0, 0, 1), c = (0, 13/16, 1/2). Measured order 2.02. Held-out error per problem: pendulum 0.00474, dc_motor 0.0276, rc_thermal 0.0461, quaternion 0.0191.

Two caveats. Elites are chosen by held-out error from 141,364 archived candidates, so the best values carry selection bias, although the optimizer itself only sees search-set error. And a tier is a mechanical comparison with the cell's incumbent when the record is written, not a validation grade: the best cell is unreplicated, and 5 of the 16 discovered elites are heldout_verified.

2Floor rounding reorders the classical methods

Every Q15 multiply rounds toward negative infinity, as ARM's ASRS does, so the error from each product leans the same way. At these budgets that bias piles up faster than truncation error shrinks. The counterfactual reruns the same 4 methods, budget and problems with round-to-nearest.

Per problem: floor against round-to-nearest

On the three search problems, floor puts euler first (RMS 0.0129) and rk4 last (0.059, 4.6× worse). Round-to-nearest restores the textbook order, with rk4 ahead of euler (0.0289 against 0.0469). Per problem, the best method changes on 5 of the 7 problems.

Dahlquist shows the mechanism most clearly. Under floor, euler, heun2 and rk38 all report error 4.54e-05, which is the entire reference value: the Q15 state decays to exactly zero, the true answer is below one LSB, and floor's downward bias lands on it. Under round-to-nearest the state stalls above zero and the error is up to 2,032× larger.

3Where quantization overtakes truncation

The premise was tested before the search started. If truncation error dominated roundoff at practical step sizes, classical analysis would already give the right answer and the search would be pointless. The falsification run swept the step size on damped_osc, computing each method's error twice, in Q15 and in float64 over exactly the same steps, so the gap between the curves is the arithmetic alone.

rk4's Q15 error bottoms out at 0.00113, separates from the float curve at h = 0.156, and climbs back to 0.0258 by h = 0.00977: below the crossover, more steps mean more floored multiplies and less accuracy. heun2 separates at h = 0.0391. Both crossovers fall inside the practical step-size range, as the premise needed.

The stored verdict is "mixed": coefficient arithmetic is 50 to 56% of per-step cycles under the fast multiplier, above the 30% proceed line, but heun2 under the slow one sits at 14.8%, just under the 15% kill line. At the shared budget under floor, rk4 is the best of the 4 methods on none of the 7 problems.

4The rc_thermal quantization floor

rc_thermal is the archive's stiff problem: a three-state RC network decaying toward equilibrium, with its derivative stored at DERIV_SCALE = 1/8 and one LSB worth 0.000122 in physical units. All 8 classical methods report errors between 0.1558 and 0.1582, a spread of 1.6%. They agree because every one of them collapses.

Each method drives the Q15 state to within 5 to 18 LSBs of the origin (euler ends at raw state (14, 15, 17), rk4 at (4, 4, 6)) while the true solution still has norm 0.1592 at t = 4, so the reported error is the reference itself. Late in the decay the scaled derivative terms are a few LSBs, and floor turns them into extra decay.

It is a floor effect, not a limit of Q15: under round-to-nearest rk38 reaches 0.0511, and the best discovered method on this problem, a 2-stage, order-2 tableau at 17 cycles per step, reaches 0.0205 while still using floor.

5Phase 0, closed by enumeration

Phase 0 is the one part of the space small enough to close completely: two-stage methods with a21 on a dyadic lattice, 256 candidates, of which 16 have exactly representable b weights. All 16 were enumerated, verified and archived, so the result holds over the whole space rather than a sample.

The optimum is a21 = −1/8 with b = (5, −4): held-out error 0.0613 at 15 cycles per step. The runner-up, a21 = −1/4, is at 0.0614, 0.04% away, so call it a tie. Neither is a textbook method, and both have lower held-out error than all 8 classical methods (the best of those is midpoint at 0.0844). Within the family, midpoint ranks 7 and heun2 12 of 16.

6Practical problems nobody tuned for

Everything above uses the archive's own search and held-out problems. The validation suite asks whether the winners transfer to 5 equations from embedded applications (power electronics, battery management, vehicle dynamics / ADAS, communications / clocking and biomedical devices) that no optimizer saw, run at the same 65,536-cycle budget in Q15 with floor rounding and scored against independent reference solutions.

The discovered methods have the lower error on 4 of the 5 problems, with a median error ratio (best discovered over best classical) of 0.475. The widest margin is on buck_converter (0.00198 against 0.0208 for rk38, 10.5×). The three-stage champion from finding 1, coefficients unchanged, wins 3 of them. On pll_lock, rk38 reaches 0.0128 against 0.0159 for the best discovered method (ratio 1.24), so the transfer is strong but not universal.

Float64 runs of the same tableaus and step counts are at least 337× more accurate in every case, so these errors measure quantization, and the largest raw Q15 value in any run is 20,130 of 32,767, so none leaned on overflow luck. The 3 stiff problems tell a different story (overflow, not accuracy); they and the full tables are on the findings validation page.

Discovered, classical and library methods

The chart shows one column of the full matrix folded below it; no single column ranks the methods.

At matched tolerance the float64 libraries are far more accurate than any Q15 method (median error ratio, best Q15 over best library, 519), and at identical step counts float64 rk4 has the lower error in all 56 comparable cells. That is the price of 16-bit floor arithmetic. The Q15 methods offer bounded integer arithmetic at a fixed cycle cost known in advance, which no library here provides, and within that regime the discovered methods have the lowest held-out error (finding 1). The stiff column is the weak spot of the whole explicit class: an expensive tableau takes large steps at a fixed budget, leaves the stability region and overflows. That failure is the argument for the implicit class.

Measured speed

The benchmark checks the cycle model against a clock: it runs the champion and rk4 through the same pinned Q15 solver on all 7 scored problems, so only the tableau differs and the per-step time ratio isolates what the coefficients cost.

Every problem lands within 22% of the predicted ratio. At the same budget the champion also has the lower error on 5 of the 7 problems (rk4 keeps dahlquist and pendulum; median error ratio 0.35), so the time saved per step does not cost accuracy. Python timings check the model rather than stand in for the chip; the protocol and its caveats are on the findings validation page.

How the numbers were made

Code assigns each record's tier when it is written (heldout_verified, search_only or unreplicated) by comparison with its cell's incumbent. The model proposes hypotheses only as predicates in a closed grammar, and code writes the verdict: no data or an effect below Cohen's d of 0.2 is inconclusive, and refuted hypotheses are fed back into later prompts. The premise got the same treatment, a falsification run with thresholds fixed in advance and its verdict published either way (finding 3).

Limits

The cycle model is analytic and has never been checked against a physical chip inside the loop. Measured order comes from one scalar problem in float64. Error is judged at the final time, not along the trajectory, and floor is one hardware rounding convention among several. Optimality claims from the enumerated phases hold only within their lattices, and four held-out problems are a strong filter but not a statistical guarantee. The full list, with the reasoning, is on the findings methodology page.

The other two classes

Everything on this page is the explicit class. The implicit and adaptive classes are measured in float64, outside the scored archive, and their results are on the findings site's implicit and adaptive pages.
